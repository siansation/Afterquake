After an earthquake, what should you do next?

AfterQuake helps you understand aftershocks and stay connected with your group.

  1. Choose an earthquake on the globe, or follow the Türkiye story.
  2. Pick your place on the map, then press Read summary for a short estimate and next steps.
  3. Check in to let your group know how you are. These posts do not contact emergency services.

        About these estimates

        AfterQuake estimates further shaking using public USGS earthquake data. It cannot inspect a building or give an all-clear. Early data can change. Check the selected date when exploring past earthquakes.

        For safety instructions, follow local officials. Our short guidance draws on USGS earthquake guidance and CDC guidance after an earthquake.

        How the forecast at your spot works

        1. How many. First, we fit a Reasenberg–Jones aftershock model to events at or before your selected time. This prototype uses one prior everywhere, while USGS operational forecasts use regional parameters and more detailed handling of missing events.

        2. Where. We then use past epicentres as proxies for future source locations, with a 6 km distance smoothing term and a fixed 10 km depth. Together, these form a simplified spatial model.

        3. How hard. To estimate shaking at your spot, we use the Allen–Wald–Worden (2012) active-crust intensity equation, including its scatter, and integrate over a normalised M5–8.5 magnitude distribution. This leaves out smaller aftershocks and sources beyond 400 km.

        4. Your ground. We also account for local ground conditions with a soil term fitted to ShakeMaps: ΔMMI = × log10(Vs30/760).

        5. Your building. We choose a damage threshold from the Modified Mercalli scale or, for "typical local building", from the country's USGS PAGER casualty model. To represent possible weakening, the threshold drops one level if mainshock shaking here was near that level, or if the ground-failure model gives at least 10% liquefaction.

        6. Time period. For your chosen stay, we integrate the aftershock rate over the whole 5-minute to 24-hour interval. We then project when the chance during that interval falls below your limit, assuming the model continues unchanged.

        7. Live earthquakes. For quakes opened from the globe, we use the same model with the USGS catalogue, ShakeMap and PAGER report. All three are checked for revisions every 2 minutes while the quake is open. Ground-failure maps are not loaded, and because early ShakeMaps are often automatic, later revisions can change the estimate. This is not an official USGS forecast.

        Replay limits (case studies). Although the timeline filters earthquake origin times, the catalogue contains later revisions, the mainshock ShakeMap may have been processed years later, and the soil coefficient was fitted using these sequences. The replay therefore illustrates past sequences; it cannot reconstruct a real-time forecast or independently validate local shaking.

        Using the result. These are prototype estimates, with local detail limited by the input grids. Whether a building is safe to enter still needs a structural inspection and local authority guidance.

        How the estimates have been checked

        Does it forecast how many aftershocks?

        To check the rate model, we make seven-day regional M5+/M6+ forecasts at 0.5–60 days after each mainshock, using only events with earlier origin times. Because the catalogue contains revisions and the forecast windows overlap, these are retrospective, correlated checks. They do not validate local shaking or building damage.
